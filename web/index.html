Two things go home with the patient

Both end at the same place: the hospital can see, and the patient can show, what was really done.

1. Proof the breathing exercise was done

After big surgery, patients go home with a plastic breathing exerciser (an incentive spirometer) and are told to use it every hour. At home, nobody can see if they do. A clip-on unit reads each breath, a voice coach guides it, and the nurse's list shows who needs a call.

The spirometer stays the hero: it is the part that proves the exercise. The clip-on unit is in development; the breaths on this site are simulated.

2. The doctor's prescription, on the patient's phone

The doctor writes the prescription in the hospital software. The patient sees every medicine on their own phone, in plain words and in their language. At the pharmacy counter they show that screen. No paper is needed.

The prescription starts as digital data, so nothing has to be decoded from a photo. Sample data. Not tried with a real pharmacy.

How it works in 3 steps

  1. The doctor sets the plan

    In the hospital software the doctor writes the breathing plan and the prescription. The nurse sees the patient on her list.

  2. The patient has it all on their phone

    A breathing coach in their language, every medicine in plain words, the next visit. At the pharmacy counter they show the prescription screen.

  3. The hospital sees what happened

    The nurse sees who is on track and who needs a call. A patient's tap is shown as "reported"; only a sensor reading counts as "measured".

Safety is in the code, not left to the AI

  • The AI never diagnoses and never advises on a medicine, a dose or a time.
  • Danger words (for example chest pain) get one fixed message: "Please contact your nurse now. In an emergency call 112."
  • A named person confirms every prescription line before any reminder exists.
  • The family sees anything only with the patient's separate consent.
