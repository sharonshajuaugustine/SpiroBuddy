1. Problem, solution and three tiers

The problem

After big surgery the patient goes home. Nobody can see whether the breathing exercise is done, and the medicines are on a paper that is easy to lose or misread.

The solution

Connect the hospital to the patient's phone. Show that the exercise was done. Put the doctor's prescription on the patient's own screen, to show at the pharmacy counter, with no paper.

Three tiers plan 11 §3.1

Tier 1 · Hospital spirometer

The clip-on unit, the voice coach, the nurse list and the family update. The hospital pays, about ₹1,000 per patient episode Estimate.

Tier 2 · Recovery software

The doctor's prescription on the phone, medicine reminders, visits and papers. Hospital or family pays monthly; not priced yet.

Tier 3 · Premium home kit

Tiers 1 and 2, plus a lent hub phone, optional lent Bluetooth devices (pulse oximeter, BP cuff) and planned nurse check-in calls. Families already pay ₹1,000-3,500 a day for a home nurse; we are testing whether about ₹2,000 for a 30-day kit is a price they accept Estimate plan 11 · line 1 · R2.

3. Where we would start

Kerala, heart-surgery units, with one teaching hospital and one mission or private hospital. B1-13

  • Why Kerala: a rule that lets registered startups sell to government without a tender, up to ₹50 lakh; strong nurse numbers, so someone can hand out the unit; many mid-size private and mission hospitals; and a busy public heart unit to pilot in (Kottayam: 1,033-1,364 open-heart operations a year). B1-13 · S19, S12
  • Why heart surgery: the stay is long (10 days built into the government scheme's package), the exercise is routine, and the reminder trial that anchors our evidence was in bypass patients.

The no-tender rule rests on one source and we could not confirm it is unchanged in October 2026. Confirm with Kerala Startup Mission before relying on it. If the team is based in Karnataka or Tamil Nadu, start at home instead.

4. Roadmap

All of this is a plan. plan 11 §3.3 · E-20

0-3 months

Plan
  • Bench-test one unit against the spirometer's printed marks.
  • Hold 5-10 interviews, including "who confirms prescriptions on your ward?" and a priced-kit question.
  • Ask CDSCO in writing, through MedTech Mitra, about the unit's class and the medicine module.
  • Build and test the doctor-writes-it prescription flow on synthetic prescriptions only.

To move on: the bench test passes; at least 3 of 10 families or nurses say yes to a priced kit; a written CDSCO answer is requested.

3-12 months

Plan
  • Run the spirometer pilot (about 50 patients, one hospital, a second ward later).
  • Add medicine reminders for pilot patients from doctor-confirmed schedules. No AI reading until an ethics committee allows it.
  • Prototype a bracket for the three-ball spirometer. Send the episode summary into the hospital's own system.

To move on: the share of due sessions done is measured; zero unconfirmed schedules were sent; nurse time per patient is measured.

12-24 months

Plan
  • The premium home kit with lent hub phones and Bluetooth devices.
  • A link to India's national health-records network, and paid hospital or family pilots in the ₹2,000 band Estimate plan 11 §3.3.
  • Decide on a base unit with its own SIM for homes without a smartphone.

To move on: paying customers renew; the error rate is measured; the licence path is clear. After that (18-36 months): licence granted, 5-10 paying sites, a controlled study, a post-TB pilot with a basic-phone mode.

5. What stays hard to copy, and the honest risks

Harder to copy

  • A sensor reading is a measurement; a patient's tap is only a report. We keep the two apart.
  • The whole loop for India: home use, Indian languages, the doctor's prescription on the phone, a nurse list and consented family updates in one place.
  • Safety rules that are code and tests, not promises.

The idea of tracking a spirometer is proven in US hospitals (a clip-on tracker was cleared by the US FDA in August 2025). We add what India needs. PITCH_FACTS #21, #23

Not hard to copy, so we do not compete on it

  • Records, booking and reminders: the government now gives these away free (Aarogya Setu 2.0, launched 29 June 2026). plan 11 · line 3 · R6
  • A paid home-recovery package: a large hospital group already sells one. plan 11 · line 2 · R15

So we plan to link to national records, not build another records app.

Risks we name ourselves

  • The three-ball spirometer. Our sensor reads the piston type only, and many Indian wards seem to use the three-ball type. So we would supply the right spirometer with the unit. E-12 · not confirmed
  • The device is not built. The software is at readiness level 4 and the device at 3. Breaths in the demo are simulated. PITCH_PLAN · TRL
  • Regulation. We expect the clip-on unit to be Class B and will confirm with CDSCO before any patient. PITCH_FACTS #35
  • Evidence. The exerciser on its own has weak evidence for cutting lung complications. We measure whether it is done; we claim no outcome. PITCH_FACTS #33
  • The paperless pharmacy. No chemist has been asked to accept a phone screen, and the check-by-QR is still in development.
  • Paths we would drop if the evidence does not appear: insurers, direct sale to families, sickle cell, and post-COVID rehab. E-20

6. What we are asking mentors for

A pilot site

An introduction to one heart-surgery or physiotherapy team willing to run a 30-50 patient pilot.

A clinical adviser

A surgeon, nurse or physiotherapist to check the care plan, the wording and the safety rules.

Regulatory guidance

Help with the CDSCO steps, the ethics committee route, and a startup grant application.

8. Works beside your hospital's software

The short answer

  • SpiroBuddy sits beside the hospital's existing software. It does not replace it. The hospital keeps its own system; we add the part the patient uses at home.
  • In from the hospital: who the patient is, the operation, the discharge date and the doctor in charge. Later, prescriptions and visits.
  • Out to the hospital: the breathing summary, medicine doses as reported by the patient, alerts and what was done about them, visit outcomes, documents, the consent record and the audit trail.
  • One safety rule never bends: an imported prescription is only a draft. No reminder exists until a named doctor, or a nurse the hospital has allowed, confirms it.
  • No lock-in: a full export on request. Moving a hospital in is meant to take one day Target, not yet tried with any hospital.

9. Drug-interaction checks – deliberately not built (would be clinical decision support)

Checking one medicine against another, or a dose against a safe range, is clinical decision support. A wrong answer could hurt a patient, and software that interprets or recommends is treated as a medical device. So SpiroBuddy does not do it.

It shows what the doctor wrote, word for word. It never changes a drug, a dose, a time or a length of treatment, and the AI never advises. plan 11 · line 7 · B4-1

If we ever build it, it needs a clinical adviser, the regulator's written view and a drug database kept by pharmacists.
