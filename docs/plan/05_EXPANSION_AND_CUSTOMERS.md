## Answer in 12 lines

1. **The first customer is one department, not "hospitals".** It is the heart-surgery unit of a mission or trust teaching hospital in the team's own state, reached through its physiotherapy head. Named options are in the target table (E-1).
2. **A big heart unit needs about 50–70 units; a 150-bed general hospital needs about 20.** The rule: units = patients a month × 0.68 (E-5). One government unit (Kottayam) or one mission unit (CMC Vellore) would each enrol roughly 70–90 patients a month.
3. **The biggest product risk is the spirometer itself.** Indian trade listings are almost all the 3-ball "flow" type, which our sensor cannot read: 23 of 28 items on IndiaMART's category page were 3-ball and none was the volume type (E-12). No real market-share figure exists. **Ask this in the first interview.**
4. **So the offer must include the spirometer.** Supply the volume-type spirometer with the unit (it costs ₹146–1,067, #7 #8) instead of assuming the ward already stocks it.
5. **The hospital's gain is unproven.** At ₹500 a patient, a 1,000-patient unit pays ₹5 lakh a year and breaks even if the average stay falls by about a fifth of a day. No study shows SpiroBuddy does that (E-6).
6. **Under a fixed government package a saved bed-day is the hospital's money. For a self-paying patient it can be lost revenue,** unless the bed is refilled. Lead with full hospitals and package patients.
7. **Best expansion paths, in order:** (a) more surgery types in the same hospital, (b) pre-surgery practice ("prehabilitation"), (c) post-acute rehab chains and home-care firms, (d) lung rehab after TB and for COPD, (e) other devices, (f) other countries (E-9 to E-15).
8. **Evidence is thin outside surgery too.** Sickle cell: one 1995 trial of 29 patients was positive; a 2025 review of three small trials found no clear benefit (E-9). Rib fractures: one 50-patient trial was strongly positive (E-9). Pre-surgery breathing-muscle training has the best evidence of all, but it uses a different device (E-13).
9. **Languages: the current 10 Indian languages are the mother tongue of about 87.8% of Indians.** Add Urdu (4.19%) and Assamese (1.26%) next to reach about 93.3% (E-16).
10. **NABH (the hospital accreditation body) gives us a selling hook, not a requirement.** Its 6th edition, in force for new applications since 1 Jan 2025, stresses patient education, digital tools and patient-reported outcomes (E-4). We could not read the standard's exact clause numbers.
11. **What stays hard to copy:** a labelled set of real Indian patients' breaths, working ward relationships, the returns-and-cleaning routine, and the device licence. The voice library is the easiest part to copy (E-18).
12. **First three partnerships to seek:** one teaching hospital with a clinical champion; the spirometer maker Romsons; one post-acute rehab chain (Sukino or HCAH) (E-19).

**How a pilot becomes a line in next year's budget (our reasoning)**
1. Ask at the start: "When does your financial year's budget close?" Indian companies mostly run April to March, so department budget requests are usually written in the months before April. Confirm the month with each hospital.
2. Time the pilot to end at least two months before that.
3. Have the department head put it in the department's request as a **running cost per patient**, not as equipment. A per-patient fee needs no capital approval (02, B2-19).
4. Give the quality officer a reason to back it (E-4) and the finance head the arithmetic (E-6).
5. Get the first order as a one-year rate contract with a monthly usage report. That report is the renewal case.

## E-5: How many patients per customer, and how many units

**The rule (our arithmetic).** Each patient holds a unit about 14 days. Add about 3 days to get it back, cleaned and re-issued: 17 days. So units in use = patients a month × 17 ÷ 30 = × 0.57. Add 20% spares for losses, faults and late returns: **units needed ≈ patients a month × 0.68.** The 3 days and the 20% are assumptions; the pilot replaces them.

| B. Mission teaching hospital | CMC Vellore: over 900 open-heart plus over 200 closed-heart and lung operations a year (N7) | 1,100 ÷ 12 = 92; × 80% (**assumption**) | 73 | **about 50** |

## E-6: The economics from the hospital's side

**Plainly: no study shows that SpiroBuddy shortens stay, prevents a complication, cuts readmissions or saves nurse time. Everything below is "what would have to be true", using numbers already in the repo.**

| Possible gain | What we can say with a source | What we cannot say |
|---|---|---|
| Bed-days | A ward bed-day costs an Indian hospital about ₹2,307 and an ICU day about ₹25,927 (#141, #142). At CMC Vellore, heart-surgery patients with a lung complication stayed 17.6 days against 7.6 (#169) | That we shorten any stay |
| Fixed-package margin | The government scheme pays ₹1,62,400 for a bypass with a 10-day stay built in (#128). Extra days come out of the hospital's pocket | That we reduce extra days |
| Readmissions | No Indian 30-day figure for bypass exists (report §2.6) | Anything |
| Nurse time | Not measured. Dozee's own nurse-time claims vary from 30 minutes to 2.5 hours a patient-day (02, B2-11, B2-12) | Any number of minutes |
| Patient satisfaction | A caregiver-support trial halved unplanned visits (#158) | That scores rise |
| Accreditation evidence | NABH's 6th edition stresses patient education and patient-reported measures (E-4) | "Meets NABH" |
| Marketing value | 32–48% of surveyed executives rate "innovativeness" a top buying criterion (01, B1-2) | A rupee value |

**Illustrative return – a heart unit doing 1,000 operations a year (illustrative only)**
| Line | Arithmetic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cost at ₹500 a patient | 1,000 × ₹500 | ₹5,00,000 a year |
| Break-even in ward bed-days | ₹5,00,000 ÷ ₹2,307 | 217 bed-days = **0.22 day per patient** |
| Break-even in ICU days | ₹5,00,000 ÷ ₹25,927 | 19 ICU days = **one ICU day per 52 patients** |
| Break-even in complications avoided | One complication = 10 extra days (CMC Vellore) × ₹2,307 = ₹23,070 at ward cost only; ₹5,00,000 ÷ ₹23,070 | **About 22 fewer complications per 1,000 patients** (2.2 percentage points) |
| At ₹200 a patient (software only, hospital owns units: 06) | 1,000 × ₹200 = ₹2,00,000 | 0.09 day per patient, or about 9 complications per 1,000 |

Three honest limits:
1. **A saved bed-day is only worth money if the bed is refilled, or the patient is on a fixed package.** For a self-paying or privately insured patient billed per day, a shorter stay can *reduce* the hospital's income. So the argument is strongest in full hospitals and for package patients. (Our reasoning.)
2. The ₹23,070 joins two different studies (a cost survey and one hospital's stay data). It is a floor, since some of those days are in ICU.
3. Whether adherence coaching changes complications at all is unknown. The best related trial raised use and slightly improved an X-ray score (#2); a Korean reminder-plus-dashboard trial found no benefit (#52).

- Status: inputs CONFIRMED in the ledger; the calculation is **illustrative, NOT CONFIRMED**.
- What it means for SpiroBuddy: sell the pilot as "we will measure this for you". Put the break-even line on a slide as a question, not a claim: "Would a fifth of a day be plausible on your ward?"

Evidence quality low to moderate; none of the trials could hide who got the training (N13, A). **This is a different device from ours**: it makes breathing in harder on purpose. Do not borrow its numbers for the incentive spirometer.
- Buyer: same hospital; the pre-admission clinic.
- Product change: enrol at booking instead of discharge; a longer loan (so more units, see row F in E-5). To use the evidence properly we would need to read a breathing-muscle trainer (E-13).
- Regulation: unchanged for the spirometer.
- When: 6–18 months, as an add-on at a pilot site.

## E-12: The flow-type (3-ball) question – the finding that matters most
- **Detail.** Our sensor measures how high one piston rises, so it reads only the **volume** type. The 3-ball **flow** type has three light balls in three short tubes; there is no single height to measure.
- **No market-share data exists.** We found no survey, tender summary or industry figure splitting Indian spirometer use by type.
- **Trade signals all point the same way:**
  - IndiaMART's "Respirometer, Respiratory or Lung Exerciser" category page listed 28 products: **23 three-ball, 3 single-ball, 0 volume type**, priced ₹48–175 each wholesale (N4, C).

  - The government's own online marketplace calls the category "Respirometer / Respiratory Exerciser"; one Andhra Pradesh bid was for 1,753 of them (2022: snippet only, C).

- **The other side:** a Manipal paper says both types are in "routine practice" (snippet only); US guidance suggests the volume type (#42); a Mangaluru trial found better walking gains with it (#89).

## E-17: Data and research as a product
- **What could be offered:**
  1. **To the hospital:** a monthly adherence and patient-education report for its quality file (E-4). Sold as part of the service, not separately.
  2. **To medical colleges:** a ready measuring tool for student and faculty research on breathing exercise. In return: co-authored papers, which are our evidence.
  3. **A registry:** pooled, anonymised breath-by-breath data across sites. India has no national heart-surgery registry (01, S2), so even basic recovery data is scarce.
  4. **To device makers or pharma:** summary statistics on how devices are really used.
- **The limits (our reading of sources already in the repo; confirm with a lawyer):**
  - The hospital, not SpiroBuddy, decides how patient data is used when it is the customer. We act on its instructions (06, B6-14; 04).
  - Research use needs ethics-committee approval and the patient's informed consent, with a literate witness where needed (05). Consent for care is not consent for research or for resale.
  - "Anonymised" must be real: no names, phone numbers, dates of birth or rare combinations that point to one person. Voice recordings are personal data.
  - Only the patient can consent. A son or daughter cannot (#122).
  - India's AI-in-healthcare guidance expects models to be tested on real, representative data (05). Ours has seen only practice breaths.
  - Never sell data that could identify a person. Never use pilot data for marketing without written permission.
- Status: **NOT CONFIRMED** as a business; the limits are our reading.
- What it means for SpiroBuddy: treat data as how we earn evidence and trust in years 1–3, not as revenue. Write the registry idea into the first pilot's consent form and protocol, or the data cannot be reused later.

## What NOT to claim
- **"We have partners, customers or interested hospitals."** Nobody on any list here has been contacted.
- **"Works with any spirometer."** It reads only the volume type.
- **"X% of Indian hospitals use the volume type"** or any share. No data exists.
- **"Prevents acute chest syndrome in sickle cell disease"**, or any disease-prevention claim. The 2025 review found no clear benefit; health-claim law also applies (04).
- **"Cuts pneumonia by half."** That Cochrane figure is for pre-surgery breathing-muscle training with a different device.
- **"NABH compliant", "NABH approved", "required by NABH".**
- **"Saves the hospital ₹X"** or "pays for itself". The break-even in E-6 is a question, not a result.
- **"Insurers will pay"**, **"the TB programme will buy"**, **"we will export next year".**
- **"Covers 88% of Indians"** without "as mother tongue, Census 2011". Many elderly "Hindi" speakers use a regional variety.
- **Any yearly operation count for a named hospital as an official figure.** They are website claims.
- **"A market of 5.5 crore COPD patients."** That is a disease count, not people who would be prescribed, reached or paid for.
